Part One

Why this manual exists

Most accommodation errors in Mississippi are not acts of bad faith. They are acts of kindness applied at the wrong altitude.

A well-meaning biology teacher shortens the test from forty items to twenty because the newcomer "can't finish." A history teacher gives the student a word bank. An algebra teacher lets a bilingual peer explain the problem in Spanish. Every one of those adults believed they were helping. Two of the three just reduced what the student was expected to learn, and one of them — if repeated on a state assessment — would trigger a test irregularity investigation.

The difference between an accommodation and a modification is not a difference of generosity. It is a difference of construct. An accommodation removes the language barrier standing between the student and the content. A modification removes the content. The first is a civil right. The second, applied without disclosure, is a slow-motion denial of one.

This manual gives you three things: a defensible way to decide what goes on a Language Service Plan, a clean register of what Mississippi actually permits, and the paperwork to prove you did both. Everything here is written for a high school block where you may be the only person in the building who knows the difference.

How to use this book

Read Parts 1–6 once, in order, before your first SET meeting. Keep Parts 7–9 open on the table during the meeting. Photocopy Part 15 and never write an LSP without it.

Part Two

The legal floor

You are not offering a service. You are discharging an obligation that predates you by fifty years.

AuthorityWhat it obligates you to do
Title VI, Civil Rights Act of 1964Prohibits national-origin discrimination in federally funded programs. Failure to address a language barrier is discrimination, whether or not anyone intended it.
Lau v. Nichols (1974)Identical materials, teachers, and curriculum do not equal equal opportunity when the student cannot understand the language of instruction. Sameness is not equity.
EEOA §1703(f) (1974)Districts must take appropriate action to overcome language barriers impeding equal participation. "Appropriate action" is the statutory hook that makes accommodations non-optional.
Castañeda v. Pickard (1981)The three-prong test: (1) the program rests on sound educational theory; (2) it is implemented with adequate resources and staff; (3) it produces results, or is revised. Prong two is where undelivered accommodations become a finding.
Plyler v. Doe (1982)Immigration status is irrelevant to the right to a public education — and therefore to LSP services.
ESSA §1111(b)(2); Title IIIAll ELs must be assessed annually for English language proficiency and included in academic assessments with appropriate supports.
34 C.F.R. §200.6Accommodation decisions must be made individually by the student's team — for ELs, the LSP team. Not by grade level. Not by proficiency level. Not by default.
MS EL GuidelinesEvery identified EL must have an LSP, developed by a Student Evaluation Team (SET), reviewed and updated annually until exit, with copies to every teacher who serves the student.
MTAM (rev. 2/19/2026)The operative list. Defines universal features, accessibility features, and accommodations, and states which apply to an LSP versus an IEP or 504 Plan.
The sentence that decides most OCR cases

A plan that exists on paper but is not implemented in the classroom is, for compliance purposes, worse than no plan at all — because it documents that the district knew what the student needed and did not deliver it. Write nothing you cannot verify.

Part Three

What an LSP must contain

Mississippi requires a Language Service Plan for every identified EL. Lack of English fluency is not a disability — the LSP is a language document, not a special education document.

Required contents

  • Student demographic information and date of first enrollment in a U.S. school
  • Yearly English Language Proficiency Test scores (domain and composite)
  • Classroom accommodations and modifications
  • State testing accommodations — each specified by code and by assessment
  • Signatures of all SET members

The Student Evaluation Team

The SET convenes at the start of each academic year to develop the plan and meets quarterly to evaluate progress and adjust. Membership should include the EL specialist, at least one general education content teacher who serves the student, an administrator or designee, a counselor, and the parent or guardian — with interpretation provided in a language the family understands. Where the student is old enough to speak for himself, seat him at the table.

Distribution

A copy of the LSP goes to every teacher who works with the student — including electives, career and technical education, and the athletic staff who administer eligibility paperwork. Distribution is not courtesy; it is the evidentiary link between the plan and its implementation. Track it (Form D, Part 15).

High school reality check

A ninth grader on a seven-period rotation may sit with eight adults in a week. Eight signatures on the acknowledgment log is the difference between "the district provided services" and "the district wrote a plan."

Part Four

The Three Doors

Mississippi organizes every support into three tiers. Nearly every LSP error in the field is a support written through the wrong door.

Door One
Universal Features

Available to every student, no documentation required.

  • Highlighter, line guide, color contrast
  • Answer masking / eliminator
  • Scratch paper, embedded ruler and calculator
  • Noise-reducing headphones, stress ball
  • Flag for review; volume control

Do not write these on an LSP. Listing a universal feature makes the plan look padded and buries the supports that matter.

Door Two
Accessibility Features

For students with an identified need, determined by the SET and documented in SET minutes filed in the cumulative folder.

  • Small group; individual testing
  • Scheduled rest breaks
  • Stay-on-task reminder
  • Student reads test aloud to self
  • Word-to-word bilingual glossary

These are not "lesser." For most ELs they are the workhorses.

Door Three
Accommodations

Require documentation on an IEP, 504 Plan, or LSP. Each is coded and must be specified by assessment.

  • Extra time
  • Testing across consecutive days
  • Read-aloud of items and answer choices
  • Paper test
  • Scribe / transcription

Only a subset carries the LSP label. See Part 7.

The most common single error in Mississippi EL files

Writing the word-to-word bilingual glossary in the accommodations block of the LSP. It is an accessibility feature, decided by the SET and recorded in SET minutes. Getting the door wrong doesn't deny the student the tool — but it does mean the documentation trail is in the wrong file when a monitor asks for it.

The authorization question

Before you write anything, answer: who has the authority to grant this, and where does the evidence live?

Part Five

The Four Lanes

Every accommodation and accessibility feature in Mississippi travels in one of four lanes. Naming the lane forces you to name the barrier.

T  Lane One
Timing

Changes how much time is allowed and how it is organized.

Barrier it answers: the student processes a second language more slowly than a first — decoding, translating, re-encoding. Time is the purest language accommodation there is, because it changes nothing about the task.

S  Lane Two
Setting

Changes the environment or how it is structured.

Barrier it answers: cognitive load. A newcomer working in a second language has no spare attention for a loud room, and often carries affective filters that a small group lowers.

P  Lane Three
Presentation

Changes how directions and information reach the student.

Barrier it answers: receptive language. This is the highest-risk lane — presentation supports are the ones most likely to cross into modification, because changing how something is presented can quietly change what is being measured.

R  Lane Four
Response

Changes how the student demonstrates learning.

Barrier it answers: productive language. The student knows the mitochondria answer and cannot spell it. Response supports separate content knowledge from English output.

Lane discipline

If you cannot name the lane, you have not named the barrier — and if you have not named the barrier, you are guessing. Write the lane letter beside every entry on the plan. It takes four seconds and it makes the logic of the plan legible to the next person who opens the folder.

Part Six · The signature idea

The Red Line

There is one line in this work. Above it, you change the path to the content. Below it, you change the content. Everything in this manual exists to keep you above the line — or to make you disclose it when you go below.

Above the line — ACCOMMODATION

Changes procedures or materials so the student can access the same grade-level expectation. The construct is untouched.

  • Extra time to read a passage — the passage is unchanged
  • A word-to-word glossary — the student still supplies the reasoning
  • Testing in a small group — the items are identical
  • Scribing a dictated response verbatim — the ideas are the student's
  • Sentence frames for oral rehearsal before an independent written response
↓  The Red Line — below here, the expectation itself changes   ↓
Below the line — MODIFICATION

Changes, lowers, or reduces the state-required learning expectation. The construct is altered.

  • Fewer objectives, fewer standards, or an easier text on the same standard
  • Answering ten of forty items; removing distractors
  • Word banks and answer choices provided on a constructed response
  • Reading the passage aloud on a reading assessment
  • Paraphrasing, explaining, or translating an item during an assessment
  • Grading on effort or participation in place of mastery

The Red Line Test — three questions

  1. Does it change what the student must know? If yes → modification.
  2. Does it change how much the student must produce or master? If yes → modification.
  3. Does it remove a language barrier while leaving the content demand fully intact? If yes → accommodation.

All three must resolve cleanly. A support that passes questions 1 and 2 but fails 3 isn't a modification — it's usually just noise, and it doesn't belong on the plan either.

Modifications on a state assessment

Providing a modification during a state accountability assessment constitutes a test irregularity. It invalidates the score and triggers a state investigation of the school's or district's testing practices. There is no version of this that ends well for the student, the teacher, or the building.

When a modification is genuinely warranted

Modifications are not forbidden in instruction. They are forbidden in silence. If the SET determines a modification is necessary in the classroom, three things must follow: (1) it is written on the LSP in the modifications block, not the accommodations block; (2) the student and family are told plainly, in their language, that this support will not be available on state assessments; and (3) the plan states how the student will be moved off it. Modification without an exit ramp is tracking.

The grading corollary

Accommodations do not change grading criteria. A student who writes a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph with extra time and a glossary is graded on the same rubric as every other student. If a teacher tells you they "gave the EL a break on the grade," you have found a modification that was never documented — and a §15 accountability problem waiting to surface.

Part Seven · Register A

LSP-eligible accommodations

These are the supports Mississippi permits an EL to receive on the basis of language alone. Verify every code against the current MTAM before a SET meeting — codes are revised.

Accommodations an EL may not claim on language alone

These carry an IEP or 504 label only. An EL receives them solely when dually identified with a disability — never because English is difficult.

CodeAccommodationWhy it is disability-only
#40Large print / online enlargementAddresses a visual access need, not a language need.

Graphic organizers are the bread and butter of sheltered instruction, and they belong in your classroom every single day. But on a state assessment, a supplemental aid is an IEP/504 accommodation. Teach the organizer relentlessly in instruction; do not write it as a state testing accommodation for a language-only EL.

Two rules that end careers

The student's primary language is not permitted on the ELPT. No glossary, no translation, no signing, no reading in Spanish. The ELPT measures English proficiency; supporting it in another language destroys the measurement.

Paraphrasing is never permitted on any assessment. Not directions, not items, not "let me say that another way." Verbatim or not at all.

The logic underneath

Two reasons a support gets struck: it interferes with what the test measures, or it alters the construct. Reading a passage aloud on a reading test does the first — you have measured listening comprehension and called it reading. Providing a Spanish glossary on the ELPT does the second — you have measured bilingual dictionary skill and called it English proficiency. Learn to run this test yourself and you will rarely need the chart.

Disability preclusion — the narrow exception

Where a disability makes assessment in an ELPT domain impossible and no appropriate accommodation exists, the IEP or 504 team may determine a preclusion for that domain. The overall score is then computed from the remaining domains, and the precluded domain counts neither for nor against the student for exit purposes. This is rare by design. Neither team may exempt an EL from the ELPT entirely or strip the EL designation.

Part Ten · The decision protocol

The ACCESS Protocol

Six gates. A support goes on the plan only if it clears all six. Run this aloud in the SET meeting — it converts a vague conversation into a defensible record.

A
Aligned to the language barrier

The support answers a documented language need in a named domain — listening, speaking, reading, or writing — not a general academic weakness, not behavior, not motivation.

Ask: which ELPT domain score sent us here, and what does the student's work show?

C
Consistently used in instruction

The student already uses this support routinely in class. A support introduced for the first time on test day is a distraction, not an accommodation — and Mississippi requires prior consistent use.

Ask: can a content teacher name the last three times this student used it?

C
Construct-safe

It does not interfere with what the assessment measures and does not alter the construct. Run the Red Line Test (Part 6) and check the prohibition matrix (Part 9).

Ask: after this support, is the student still doing the thing the standard requires?

E
Evidenced in data

Something in the file justifies it: ELPT domain scores, screener results, work samples, formative data, observation notes, or documented parent input.

Ask: if a monitor asks "why this support for this student," what document do I hand them?

S
Specified

Written with the code, the amount, the delivery method, and the assessments it applies to. "Extra time" is not a plan. "#20 Extra time — 50% additional, MAAP Algebra I and MAAP English II" is a plan.

Ask: could a substitute test coordinator implement this correctly with no phone calls?

S
Signed and shared

SET members have signed, the family has received it in an understandable language, and every teacher who serves the student has acknowledged receipt in writing.

Ask: who is holding a copy right now, and can I prove it?

The subtraction rule

A plan with four supports the student actually uses outperforms a plan with twelve that no one implements. Every accommodation you add is a promise the building has to keep seven periods a day. Add slowly. Remove without sentiment when the data says the student has outgrown it — and record the removal as growth, in front of the student.

Level 5 and monitored students

Students at Level 5 and those in the four-year monitoring window generally need no testing accommodations. What they need is attention: quarterly grade checks, teacher input on academic language demands in new courses, and a documented re-entry pathway if performance drops. Monitoring is not a filing status. It is a service.

Do not build the menu into a checkbox form

The fastest way to generate an OCR finding is a district LSP template where every Level 1 student receives an identical set of pre-checked accommodations. That is a group determination wearing an individual's name. Individualize, and let the plans differ visibly from one another.

Part Twelve

Classroom versus state testing

One sentence governs this entire relationship: every state testing accommodation must also be a classroom accommodation, but not every classroom accommodation may be used on a state test.

The wide circle
Classroom accommodations

Broad. Includes everything you do to make grade-level content reachable — visuals, native-language preview, cooperative structures, extended time, glossaries, organizers, oral response options, chunked text.

Also the place where documented modifications may legitimately live, if the SET decides they are necessary and the family is told what that means.

The narrow circle, fully inside it
State testing accommodations

Narrow. Only what appears in the current MTAM with an LSP label, specified by code and by assessment, and used consistently in class beforehand.

Nothing may appear here that has not been living in the classroom for months.

The practical consequence

You cannot decide in March that a student will get read-aloud on the MAAP. You decide in August, you deliver it every week, you log it, and in March the state assessment simply looks like a Tuesday. Accommodation is a habit before it is a form.

What to tell the family

Parents deserve to know, in their own language and in plain terms, three things: which supports their child receives in the classroom, which of those will be present on state tests, and — where a modification is in place — that their child's grades may not reflect grade-level mastery. That last conversation is uncomfortable. Have it anyway, and document that you had it. Families who are surprised at graduation are families who file complaints, and they are right to.

Part Thirteen

Dually identified students

An EL with a disability requires two plans in Mississippi: an IEP and an LSP, or a 504 Plan and an LSP. Never one standing in for the other.

The rule

  • Lack of English fluency is not a disability. Language need alone never justifies disability-based accommodations.
  • An IEP does not replace the LSP. The language obligation is independent of the disability obligation and survives it.
  • A student may not be exempted from the ELPT by an IEP team or a 504 team, and the EL designation may not be removed by either.
  • Accommodations must be reasoned separately: which barrier is this addressing — language, or disability? Then document it in the correct plan. Many will appear in both.

The sequencing question

Before referring an EL for special education evaluation, the team must be able to answer: have we ruled out language acquisition as the explanation? That requires documented, appropriate EL services delivered with fidelity over sufficient time, progress data across all four domains, native-language assessment where feasible, and educational history including interrupted schooling. Under-identification and over-identification are both civil rights failures. The defense against either is the same: a paper trail showing you asked the right question in the right order.

Where the specialist earns the room

You are frequently the only person at the eligibility table who can say, with data, "this profile is consistent with second-language acquisition at year two, not a specific learning disability" — or, just as importantly, "this is not a language issue, and we have waited long enough." Bring the ELPT domain trajectory, the work samples, and the schooling history. Say it plainly.

Part Fourteen

Fidelity, monitoring, and non-implementation

Writing the plan is one-fifth of the work. Proving it happened is the other four-fifths.

The quarterly cycle

QuarterSET actionEvidence generated
Q1 · AugustDevelop or renew the LSP; distribute to all teachers; collect acknowledgment signatures; hold the family conference with interpretationSigned LSP; distribution log; family conference note
Q2 · OctoberFirst fidelity check: two classroom observations per student focus group; collect implementation logs; review formative dataWalkthrough forms; implementation logs; grade check
Q3 · JanuaryMid-year adjustment; confirm state testing accommodations with the DTC; verify platform flags are set in the testing systemsAmended LSP if changed; DTC confirmation email
Q4 · AprilPost-ELPT review; exit determinations; plan next-year supports and monitoring statusExit documentation; monitoring roster

What fidelity looks like in a walkthrough

You are not checking whether the teacher is nice. You are checking four observable things: the accommodation was available, the student used it, it was delivered as written, and the work product shows the student was held to the same standard. Anything less is a coaching conversation, not a gotcha — start there.

When accommodations are not being delivered

Escalate in this order, documenting at every step:

  1. Coach. Assume unfamiliarity, not resistance. Model the accommodation in the teacher's own classroom with the teacher's own material. Most non-implementation ends here.
  2. Support in writing. Send a short, warm, dated email restating the specific accommodation and offering materials. You now have a record and the teacher has a resource.
  3. Document the pattern. Three dated instances across a grading period, with the specific accommodation and the observed practice. Facts only, no adjectives.
  4. Report to administration. Written notice to the principal referencing the LSP, the EEOA obligation, and the Castañeda prong-two exposure. Request a remedy and a date.
  5. District-level notice. If unremedied, written notice to the federal programs director or EL coordinator. Keep a personal, dated copy of everything.
Your own exposure

A specialist who knows accommodations are not being delivered and does not document the knowledge is in a worse position than one who never knew. Documentation is not disloyalty to colleagues. It is the only thing that converts a private frustration into a fixable institutional problem — and the only thing that protects you.

Twelve findings monitors actually write

  1. LSPs on file but no evidence of distribution to content teachers
  2. Identical accommodation sets across all students at a proficiency level
  3. State testing accommodations listed without codes or without naming the assessment
  4. Accommodations on the plan that were never used in the classroom
  5. Accessibility features written in the accommodations block; SET minutes absent
  6. Modifications recorded as accommodations, with no family notification
  7. Bilingual glossary accommodation with no approved dictionary on site
  8. Read-aloud flagged for the ELPT Reading domain or the 3rd Grade Reading Summative
  9. Missing SET signatures, particularly the parent or guardian
  10. Parent communication in English only, with no documentation of interpretation
  11. Dually identified students holding an IEP with no LSP
  12. Monitored students with no quarterly review evidence for the full four years

Glossary & sources

TermWorking definition
Accessibility featureA support that meets a specific student need without changing learning expectations. SET-determined; documented in SET minutes.
AccommodationA change in procedure or material that ensures equitable access and produces valid results. Documented on an IEP, 504 Plan, or LSP. Never reduces learning expectations.
ConstructThe hypothesized ability an assessment is designed to measure. If a support alters the construct, it is not an accommodation.
ELPTEnglish Language Proficiency Test — Mississippi's annual K–12 proficiency assessment across Listening, Speaking, Reading, and Writing.
Embedded / non-embeddedEmbedded supports live inside the online testing platform. Non-embedded supports are supplied locally by the school.

MAAPMississippi Academic Assessment Program.
ModificationA change that lowers or reduces state-required learning expectations. Prohibited on state assessments; permitted in instruction only with documentation and family notification.

Facilitator overview

What this session has to accomplish

Your faculty does not need to memorize the MTAM. They need to leave able to do four things they cannot currently do.

Outcomes

  1. Sort any support into the correct tier — universal feature, accessibility feature, or accommodation — and name who authorizes it.
  2. Apply the Red Line Test to distinguish an accommodation from a modification in under ten seconds.
  3. Name the four supports Mississippi permits an EL on the basis of language alone, and the two prohibitions that invalidate a state test.
  4. Locate the accommodations on a real LSP and describe what those look like in their own classroom on Monday.

What to prepare

  • Printed: participant packet (the activity pages of this module), one Red Line manual per table, one blank LSP template per participant
  • Physical: a length of red painter's tape down the center of a table or wall — you will be sorting cards across an actual red line
  • Cut in advance: the twelve sorter statements from Activity 2, one set per table
  • On hand: the current MTAM (open on the projector), an approved word-to-word bilingual dictionary, and one deidentified LSP from your building
The tone to set in the first thirty seconds

Nobody in the room broke a rule on purpose. Say that out loud, early, and mean it. The moment a content teacher believes this session is about catching them, you lose the next eighty-nine minutes. This is a session about a line most people were never shown.

0:00 – 0:08

Hook · Three teachers, three kindnesses

Read these aloud. Give tables ninety seconds. Ask: which of these three did something that would invalidate a state test score?

Teacher One
The biology teacher

Maritza is a newcomer from Honduras, four months in the country. The unit test is forty items. Ms. Blake gives her the twenty items she thinks Maritza has the best chance at, and grades out of twenty.

Teacher Two
The algebra teacher

Same student. Mr. Ruiz gives Maritza the full test, a word-to-word Spanish–English dictionary, and until the end of the school day to finish it.

Teacher Three
The history teacher

Same student. Mrs. Coy reads each question aloud to Maritza and, when Maritza looks confused, rephrases it in simpler English.

The reveal — at 0:06, not before

Teacher One modified. She reduced what the student was required to know, and the grade no longer means what a grade means.

Teacher Two accommodated, correctly, in both lanes — timing and response — and did not touch the construct.

Teacher Three did both. Reading questions aloud is a legitimate accommodation. Rephrasing is paraphrasing, and paraphrasing is never permitted on any assessment. On a MAAP administration, that is a test irregularity.

The line to say next: "All three of them were being kind. Two of them cost the student something. That's what the next eighty minutes is about."

Appendix

Questions you will be asked

Prepared answers for the four that come up in every faculty room.

"So I just can't help them?"

You can help enormously — and most of what helps is instruction, not accommodation. Pre-teaching vocabulary with images, chunking complex text, modeling with think-alouds, structured oral rehearsal before writing, visuals paired with every key term. None of that requires a plan and none of it approaches the line. The line only governs what happens on a graded measure of what the student knows.

"How am I supposed to give one kid extra time when I have twenty-eight others?"

Real problem, and worth taking seriously rather than deflecting. Practical answers: build assessments that most students finish in two-thirds of the period; establish a standing arrangement for finishing in the EL block or during an intervention period; use the small-group setting during common assessment windows. Bring the logistics to the SET — the plan should specify something the building can actually deliver seven periods a day.

"Isn't a lower-level text just meeting them where they are?"

Meeting them where they are is how you begin a lesson. It cannot be how you end one. A replacement text on the same standard means the student never encounters the grade-level expectation, and after four years of that the transcript says one thing and the student's readiness says another. The accommodation is scaffolding the grade-level text — annotate it, chunk it, front-load the vocabulary, read it together first. Same text, more support.

"What if I've been doing this wrong all year?"

Then you are in the majority, and today is when it changes. Nothing in this session is retroactive discipline. Bring the student to the next SET, get the support written correctly, and start Monday. The only version of this that becomes a problem is the one where someone knows and keeps going.

One thing not to do as facilitator

Do not name a teacher's error in the room. Ever. If someone volunteers a practice that falls below the line, thank them for the honesty, use it as the group's example without attaching it to them, and follow up privately. The willingness to say it out loud is the most valuable thing in the room, and you will only get it once.
