The 90-Minute Block
A Conductor's Guide

Five movements, one arc. What to do with ninety minutes when nobody hands you a curriculum.

Many Voices · One Storm
Part One

Structure replaces curriculum

Most EL specialists are handed a roster, a room, and no program. The instinct is to go looking for a curriculum. The better move is to build a structure so reliable that content can be poured into it from any source.

An EL block is not a content course. It is a language course that borrows content. That distinction is the whole design. Where the content comes from matters less than whether the language work is systematic — and there are four legitimate sources, usually running at once:

  • The standards and the test blueprint. The ten ELP standards and the ELPA21 task types tell you what language behaviors must be built. This is the spine.
  • A thematic progression from survival to academic. Newcomers need school, body, food, and time before they need cellular respiration. The arc is deliberate, not remedial.
  • The students' own content classes. The biology unit they are failing this week is the most motivating text in the building.
  • The students themselves. Their countries, their questions, their families, their plans. Interest is not a reward for language; it's a precondition.
The principle

If a visitor can walk into your room on any day and identify which movement you are in without asking, the structure is working. If every day looks improvised, no amount of good content will hold it together.

Part Two · The architecture

Five movements

Ninety-four minutes, five movements. The proportions matter more than the exact minutes — protect the ratio when the bell changes.

0–10
10 min
I · Entry & Language Warm-Up

A posted routine the students run without you. Sentence-frame response to a projected prompt, partner exchange, quick oral share. Attendance and materials happen underneath it.

Look for: every student speaking English within the first four minutes. Not answering — speaking.

10–35
25 min
II · Explicit Language Instruction

The one thing being taught today. A structure, a function, a set of words — named, modeled, practiced, and used. This is the only movement where the teacher talks the most, and it should still be under half the time.

Look for: a language objective posted in student-facing words, and a model the students can see, not just hear.

35–60
25 min
III · Guided Practice in Small Groups

Students at tables by need, not by seat. The teacher rotates. Levels 1–2 get more scaffolding and more repetition; Levels 3–4 get more independence and more complexity. Everyone works on the same objective at different altitudes.

Look for: the teacher sitting down. If you are standing the whole movement, you are monitoring, not teaching.

60–80
20 min
IV · Content Application

The day's language move applied to real academic content — a science text, a historical document, a math word problem, something from a content class. This is where transfer either happens or doesn't.

Look for: grade-level text with scaffolding, not below-grade text without it.

80–94
14 min
V · Output & Close

Every student produces something in English that did not exist at the start of the block — spoken, written, or both. Then a named close: what we learned, what it's for, what's next.

Look for: an artifact. Discussion is not output unless it leaves a trace you can collect.

Why output is last and non-negotiable

Comprehensible input builds the system; output forces the learner to notice the gaps in it. A block that is all input produces students who understand a great deal and can say very little — the most common failure pattern in secondary EL. Protect the last fourteen minutes even when everything else runs long.

The one folder that ends most arguments

Keep a dated growth folder per student holding one output artifact per week. When a colleague says a student "isn't making progress," or a monitor asks what services look like, you open a folder instead of making a case. Thirty-six artifacts a year is the difference between an opinion and a record.

Part Five

Installation — the first four weeks

Week 1
Names, routines, baselines
  • Learn and correctly pronounce every name
  • Teach Movement I until it runs without you
  • Collect a baseline writing and speaking sample
  • Post the block structure where students can see it
Week 2
Add the middle
  • Introduce Movements II and III
  • Form first small groups from baseline data
  • Establish partner protocols and turn-taking
Week 3
Bring in content
  • Add Movement IV using a text from a student's content class
  • Begin the growth folder
  • First contact home for every student, in the family's language
Week 4
Close the loop
  • Add Movement V and hold it
  • Run the full block start to finish
  • Invite an administrator to observe with the walkthrough crosswalk
Part Six

Walkthrough crosswalk

Hand this to your administrator before the first observation. It translates what they'll see into the framework they evaluate with.

What the administrator seesWhat it is

Two objectives postedA content objective and a language objective, per sheltered instruction design
Different tables doing different workDifferentiation by proficiency level, not by ability tracking
Students using their first languageTranslanguaging — a documented strategy for building conceptual understanding, not avoidance
Teacher seated with a small groupGuided practice at the point of need

Administrator one-pager, in one paragraph

This block runs a fixed five-movement structure so that students always know what comes next and instruction is never improvised. Every day includes explicit language instruction, small-group practice grouped by proficiency data, application to grade-level academic content, and required English output collected as evidence. Students are held to grade-level standards with scaffolding rather than given easier work. Progress is tracked by domain — listening, speaking, reading, writing — against ELPA21 growth targets, and every student has a dated growth folder available on request.

The Standards Ascent

Ten standards, three altitudes, thirty-six weeks. What each standard actually asks a student to do, and what it looks like at every level.

Many Voices · One Storm
How to read this volume

Three altitudes

Every standard is written once and taught three ways. The standard never changes. The altitude does.

Levels 1–2
Emerging Base camp

Words and phrases. Heavy visual and gestural support. Recognition before production. Success looks like a labeled drawing, a matched pair, a two-word answer.

Level 3
Progressing Ascent

Sentences becoming paragraphs. Frames provided then faded. Success looks like a claim with one piece of evidence, spoken or written.

Levels 4–5
Advanced Summit

Extended, connected discourse. Register shifts by audience. Success looks like grade-level academic writing that a content teacher would accept without qualification.

The ten

The standards, at three altitudes

The standards are not sequential

Standard 1 is not easier than Standard 10, and you do not finish one before starting the next. All ten run all year. What changes across the year is altitude, not which standard is "up."

Many Voices · One Storm
Model Block A · Levels 1–2

Describing a process

Standard 3 and Standard 9. Content borrowed from biology: the water cycle. Ninety-four minutes.

MovementWhat happens
I · 10 minProjected image of rain. Frame on the board: I see ___. The water is ___. Students write two sentences, then say both to a partner. Three volunteers share aloud.
II · 25 minTeach four sequence words — first, next, then, finally — with gesture and a four-box visual. Teach four content words with images: evaporate, condense, precipitate, collect. Model the full sequence orally twice while pointing at the diagram. Students echo, then chorally produce.
III · 25 minLevel 1 table: cut-and-paste the four stages into order, then label with the word bank. Level 2 table: same sequence, but write one sentence per stage using the frames. Teacher rotates, prompting oral production at both tables — pointing and asking What happens next?
IV · 20 minGrade-level biology textbook paragraph on the water cycle, chunked into four pieces with the diagram beside it. Students highlight the four content words where they appear in real academic prose. Partners read each chunk aloud.
V · 14 minEach student explains the water cycle in four sentences to a partner using the sequence words, then writes those four sentences. Collected for the growth folder. Close: Today you explained a process. Every science class asks you to do this.
Model Block B · Level 3

Claim and evidence

Standard 4 and Standard 6. Content borrowed from civics: should the school day start later?

MovementWhat happens
I · 10 minPrompt: What time did you wake up? How did you feel? Two sentences, partner exchange, quick tally on the board.
II · 25 minName the structure: claim, evidence, reasoning. Model with a non-academic example, then an academic one. Give the frames — I claim that ___. The evidence is ___. This matters because ___. — and a set of academic verbs: argues, suggests, indicates, demonstrates.
III · 25 minGroups take a short article on adolescent sleep and highlight three pieces of usable evidence. Teacher rotates, pushing for the reasoning link — the sentence students most often skip.
IV · 20 minStructured partner debate. Each student states a claim with evidence, then must restate the partner's claim accurately before responding. Restatement is the graded move, not winning.
V · 14 minIndependent written paragraph: claim, evidence, reasoning. Frames available but not required. Collected.
Why restatement is the graded move

Standard 6 asks students to analyze the arguments of others. Requiring accurate restatement before response makes listening comprehension visible and gradeable, and it produces a better argument than debate-as-competition. Content teachers notice this skill immediately.

The Domain &
Level Handbook

Every domain at every level, in plain English. What the student can do now, what moves them up, and what the test will ask.

Many Voices · One Storm

Twelve teaching pages

Four domains, three altitudes. Find your student, read the row, teach the move-up target.

Domain

Listening

LevelWhat the student can do nowThe move-up targetWhat the test asks
1–2 Follows one-step directions with gesture. Catches familiar words in slow, supported speech. Follow two-step directions without gesture. Identify the topic of a short exchange. Short audio, picture-choice response. Everyday and school contexts.
3 Understands the main idea of a short classroom talk. Misses detail and inference. Hold two or three details while listening. Answer a "why" question about what was said. Longer audio, multiple-choice on main idea and detail.
4–5 Follows academic discussion. Struggles with rapid speech, idiom, and implied meaning. Infer speaker purpose and attitude. Take usable notes while listening. Extended academic audio; inference and purpose items.
The move that works at every level

Listen twice. First pass for gist with no writing; second pass with a specific task. One additional exposure changes performance more than any amount of pre-teaching.

Domain

Speaking

LevelWhat the student can do nowThe move-up targetWhat the test asks
1–2 Single words and memorized phrases. Long pauses. May be silent by choice. Produce a full simple sentence unprompted. Answer a question in more than one word. Name a picture; answer a short personal question.
3 Connected sentences on familiar topics. Falters on academic content. Speak for sixty seconds on an academic topic with two supporting details. Describe a picture in detail; give a short opinion with a reason.
4–5 Extended discourse. Register and precision are the remaining gaps. Sustain an academic argument; adjust formality to audience. Extended response comparing, arguing, or explaining from a source.
On the silent period

Some newcomers say nothing for weeks or months. This is documented and normal, not defiance and not a disability. Keep providing input and low-stakes opportunities. Never force public production from a student in this stage — it raises the affective filter and extends the silence.

Domain

Reading

LevelWhat the student can do nowThe move-up targetWhat the test asks
1–2 Recognizes high-frequency words and environmental print. May decode without comprehending. Read a short simple text and answer a literal question about it. Match word to picture; short sentence comprehension.
3 Reads short informational text for main idea. Academic vocabulary blocks comprehension. Locate specific evidence in a paragraph. Use context for unknown words. Multi-paragraph passage; main idea, detail, and vocabulary items.
4–5 Reads grade-level text with effort. Slow rate; misses figurative and connotative meaning. Read at reasonable rate; analyze author's purpose and craft. Complex passages; inference, purpose, and text-structure items.
Decoding is not comprehension

A student who reads aloud fluently and understands nothing is common and frequently misidentified — sometimes as a strong reader, sometimes as learning disabled. Always check comprehension separately from oral reading. This distinction matters enormously at an eligibility meeting.

Domain

Writing

LevelWhat the student can do nowThe move-up targetWhat the test asks
1–2 Copies accurately. Labels pictures. Writes memorized phrases and simple sentences. Write two or three original connected sentences on a familiar topic. Label an image; write one or two sentences about a picture.
3 Writes a paragraph with frames. Errors don't usually block meaning. Write an organized paragraph without frames, using transitions. Short constructed response drawing on a source.
4–5 Multi-paragraph academic writing. Remaining errors are in article use, prepositions, and idiom. Produce cohesive text with varied structure and appropriate register. Extended writing integrating one or more sources.
Writing is last, and that is not a delay

Writing lags the other three domains for nearly every EL, and it is the domain most often required for exit. Do not read a low writing score as a plateau. Read it as the normal shape of acquisition — and then teach writing every single day, because it will not close on its own.

The EL Specialist's
Field Book

Field-tested moves, organized by the moment you need them. Built to be opened mid-lesson.

Many Voices · One Storm
Section One

When they won't talk

Write first, speak second.Thirty seconds of silent writing before any share-out. Production anxiety drops when there's a script in hand.
Partner before public.Never call on a student who hasn't already said it to one person. Rehearsal is the whole trick.
Give the answer, ask for the reason."The answer is evaporation. Tell me why." Removes the retrieval barrier and keeps the thinking.
Choral first.Whole-class repetition lets a student produce sound with no exposure. Often the first English a newcomer says aloud.
Count to seven.Average teacher wait time is about one second. Second-language processing needs five to seven. Count silently.
Accept the first language.A right answer in Spanish is a right answer. Take it, restate it in English, move on.
Section Two

When the text is too hard

Chunk and check.Two or three sentences at a time with a one-question comprehension check. Never assign a full page cold.
Front-load five words.Not twenty. Pick the five that block meaning and teach them with images before reading.
Read it aloud first.Listening comprehension outruns reading comprehension for most ELs. Hearing it once makes the page navigable.
Hunt cognates.Academic English is Latinate, and so is Spanish. Observe/observar. Analyze/analizar. Students find them faster than you can list them.
Number the paragraphs.Costs ten seconds. Makes every subsequent direction precise and lets students locate evidence without hunting.
Preview in the first language.Two minutes of concept preview in Spanish makes twenty minutes of English text accessible.
Section Three

When they finish early — or not at all

Same task, more evidence.Extension is depth, not volume. "Find a second piece of evidence," not "do ten more."
Promote them to teacher.Explaining to a peer is the highest-value language task in the room, and it's free.
Cut quantity, hold the standard.Three problems done well beats fifteen abandoned. Reducing volume is not modifying the expectation.
Bank the unfinished work.A dated folder of incomplete tasks is data about pacing, not evidence of laziness.
Section Four

Vocabulary

Teach the word family, not the word.Analyze, analysis, analytical. One lesson, three words, and the pattern generalizes.
Tier 2 over Tier 3.Determine, contrast, significant appear on every test in every subject. Photosynthesis appears in one unit.
Picture, not definition.Below Level 3, a dictionary definition is another text to decode. An image is instant.
Ten exposures.A word needs roughly ten meaningful encounters to stick. Plan them; don't hope for them.
Word wall they touch.A wall students physically pull cards from beats a wall they read past.
Section Five

Newcomers, first two weeks

Name first, correctly.Ask how to say it. Practice until you have it. This is the single most important thing you do all year.
Survival language before academic.Bathroom, hungry, help, I don't understand, again please. Dignity comes before content.
Assign a buddy who shares the language.For directions and logistics, not for translating instruction.
Walk the building.A map is abstract. Physically walking to the cafeteria, the nurse, and the bus line is not.
Call home in week one.In the family's language. Say something good. You are building the relationship you'll need in March.
Expect nothing in writing for a while.Some students are still finding the classroom. Grade what's there; keep providing input.
Section Six

Working with content teachers

Bring a strategy, not a complaint.Arrive with one thing that will work in their room tomorrow, made from their material.
Model in their classroom.Twenty minutes of you teaching their students beats an hour of PD about it.
Translate the data."Level 2 in reading" means nothing to them. "She can follow your lecture but can't read the chapter" is actionable.
Ask what's hardest.Open with their problem, not your mandate. You'll get a real answer and a real opening.
Follow up in writing.A short warm email after every conversation. Builds the relationship and builds the record.
Section Seven

Families

Interpretation is a right, not a favor.Provide it and document it. Never use the student as the interpreter for anything that matters.
Lead with the good news.If every call home is a problem, families stop answering. Make the first one positive.
Explain the system, not just the grade.Many families have no reference for credits, GPA, or graduation requirements here. Draw it out.
Ask about schooling history.Years attended, languages of instruction, interruptions. It changes everything about placement.
Section Eight

Data and testing

Read the domains, not the composite.Overall Level 3 hides a student at 5 in speaking and 1 in writing. The composite tells you almost nothing.
Teach the task format.Students lose points to unfamiliar formats, not missing knowledge. Rehearse the format all year.
Accommodations are a habit, not a form.Anything used on test day must have been running in class since August.
Chart growth where students see it.Movement from Level 2 to Level 3 is invisible without a graph. Make it visible; it changes effort.
Section Nine

Keeping yourself in the work

Ninety percent is the ceiling.A caseload this size means something is always undone. Choose what, deliberately, rather than failing at everything equally.
Document as you go.Five minutes a day beats a weekend in April, and it's the only thing that protects you.
Find one ally per building.One content teacher who gets it changes more than any mandate.
Keep a good-day file.Notes from students, a piece of writing that surprised you. February is long.

Block Guide

One system, any bell. Running the five movements on fifty minutes, sixty minutes, A/B days, push-in, and co-teaching.

Protect the ratio, not the minutes

The block is not ninety-four minutes. It is a proportion. When the bell changes, hold the shape and shrink everything proportionally — with one exception.

MovementShare94 min60 min50 min
I · Entry & warm-up11%1075
II · Explicit language instruction27%251613
III · Guided practice27%251613
IV · Content application21%201311
V · Output & close15%1488
The exception

Movement V does not shrink below eight minutes. Ever. On a fifty-minute period, take the time from Movements II and III instead. A block without output is a block where students listened, and listening alone does not move a proficiency level.

Configuration

Fifty minutes, daily

The tightest common schedule. Two workable approaches:

Option A
Compress all five, every day

Use the fifty-minute column above. Everything happens daily, all of it shorter. Best for Levels 1–2, where routine and repetition matter more than depth.

Cost: Movement IV gets thin. Content application becomes a single paragraph rather than a real text.

Option B
Two-day cycle

Day 1: Movements I, II, III, and a short V.
Day 2: Movements I, IV, extended III, and a full V.
Best for Levels 3–4, where sustained work with grade-level text matters most.

Cost: Requires students to hold a thread across a day. Post the two-day map where they can see it.

Configuration

A/B block — 90 minutes, every other day

You get the full architecture but only meet two or three times a week. The danger is not time; it is decay between sessions.

  • Open every block with retrieval, not review. Make Movement I a recall task from last session — students produce, rather than watch you re-teach.
  • Assign a five-minute between-days task. One sentence written, one word used, one thing noticed in a content class. Small and daily beats large and occasional.
  • Extend Movement IV to 30 minutes. With fewer sessions, each one needs to do more content work.
  • Run a two-week arc rather than a one-week arc. Six meetings, not ten. Plan the unit accordingly.
Configuration

Push-in and co-teaching

You don't control the clock. You control the language work inside someone else's lesson. The five movements still apply — they just get distributed.

MovementWhat it becomes in a content classroom
I · Warm-upArrive two minutes early. Front-load the day's three critical words with your students before the lesson starts.
II · Explicit instructionAsk the content teacher for ninety seconds to name the language demand: "Today you'll be comparing. Here are the words comparison uses."
III · Guided practicePull a small group at the table during independent work. This is where most of your real teaching happens in a push-in model.
IV · Content applicationAlready happening — it's their lesson. Your job is scaffolding access to it in real time.
V · OutputTwo minutes at the end with your students: what did you learn, said in a full sentence. Collect it.
The co-teaching conversation to have in August

Agree on three things before the first lesson: who introduces vocabulary, whether you may interrupt to clarify, and how student work will be graded. Getting these settled in August prevents the whole year from defaulting to you as an aide. Put it in writing, even informally — a shared page is enough.

Configuration

Mixed-level rooms

One period, Levels 1 through 4 in the same room. The most common secondary reality and the least written about.

  • Movements I, II, and V are whole-group. Same objective, same content, same close. Differentiate the expected output, not the topic.
  • Movement III is where the levels split. This is the entire reason guided practice exists. Protect it.
  • Movement IV uses one text at three levels of scaffolding — not three different texts. Same passage, different supports: read-aloud plus glossary for Levels 1–2, chunked with a glossary for Level 3, independent for Levels 4–5.
  • Pair across levels deliberately. A Level 4 explaining to a Level 2 gets the harder language task. Both are working.
What not to do

Do not run three separate lessons simultaneously. It's unsustainable, it fragments the room, and it means no group gets your attention for more than a few minutes. One lesson, three altitudes.
